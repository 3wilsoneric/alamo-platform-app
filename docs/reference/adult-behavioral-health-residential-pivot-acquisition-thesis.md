# Adult Behavioral-Health Residential Pivot and Acquisition Thesis

- purpose: market-environment and bed-scale framework for acquiring core operators, converting adjacent programs, and buying scarce approvals or sites
- status: decision-grade market thesis; facility-specific license, capacity, payer, clinical, and ownership diligence still required
- owners: owner, acquisition analyst, corporate development, clinical operations
- updated: 2026-09-09
- tags: acquisition, fifty-states, behavioral-health, residential, pivot, service-mix, capacity, market-fit, regulation

## Executive conclusion

For a roughly 500-bed adult behavioral-health residential platform seeking less dependence on Medicaid, the sound strategy is **not** to choose between acquisitions and service-mix conversion. It is to run two distinct lanes with different underwriting rules:

1. **Acquire core operators for dependable scale.** Buy businesses that already deliver adult, primary-mental-health residential or subacute step-down care and already have the relevant clinicians, sites, referral pathways, and operating history.
2. **Acquire adjacent operators for their operating platform and convertible beds.** The best source populations are adult SUD/co-occurring residential care and lower-acuity adult mental-health residential care, but only after the exact license, physical plant, staffing gap, payer path, and realistic convertible-bed count are known.

The central research question is therefore not “what could the company be worth?” It is: **how many adult, usable, target-fit beds does the operator control; in which markets; under what licenses; and how much of the current operation resembles the desired post-hospital residential model?** Valuation remains a later transaction screen. It should not dominate market mapping or cause estimated beds to be presented as verified capacity.

The best adjacent source asset is generally an **adult SUD residential operator with real co-occurring capability**, followed by a lower-acuity adult mental-health residential operator. Those businesses may already have 24-hour operations, medication management, payer utilization-management experience, licensed clinicians, residential real estate, and referral relationships. SAMHSA recommends integrated mental-health and substance-use treatment and specifically notes that SUD programs serving substantial co-occurring populations may need mental-health specialists and psychiatric consultation.[^1] That supports adjacency; it does not prove that an SUD license, building, staff, or payer contract can be relabeled as primary psychiatric residential care.

The regulatory research points to this order of effort:

- **Pennsylvania:** best near-term combination of explicit adult residential psychiatric license types and transaction structuring flexibility. A stock sale that leaves the licensed legal entity intact does not constitute a change of ownership under the general human-services licensing rule, although payer, corporate, and program-specific approvals still require diligence.[^2]
- **Georgia:** best clean-sheet definition of the desired product. Its new Adult Residential Mental Health Program license is expressly an adult, 24/7, short-term, non-hospital alternative used to prevent or step down from acute psychiatric hospitalization.[^3] It is an excellent market-entry thesis but a less certain acquisition shortcut because a new owner needs advance approval and the permit is location-specific.
- **Tennessee:** a practical conversion market because an operator can apply to add a license category or change bed capacity at an existing site.[^4] The regulatory pathway is visible, but the license itself appears less scarce and therefore creates less acquisition moat.
- **Florida:** a major sourcing market for adult SUD and co-occurring operators, but the label “residential treatment” is not enough. The highest-acuity adult short-term residential program sits in the public mental-health crisis framework; the ordinary adult residential treatment-facility pathway is broader and often longer-term/lower-acuity.[^5] Florida is a strong operating-company market and a conditional pivot market, not an automatic primary-MH conversion.
- **Connecticut, Massachusetts, and Washington:** potentially valuable for scarce approvals, but better treated as **acquire-the-existing-approval** markets than easy conversion markets. Their CON/DoN and hospital licensing processes can create genuine barriers, but also add transaction time, public review, and closing risk.[^6]
- **North Carolina and Minnesota:** currently poor fits for the commercial 50–150-bed pivot thesis. North Carolina has a clear psychiatric-hospital route with need-based CON, but the ordinary 24-hour residential categories do not cleanly map to the desired adult subacute product.[^7] Minnesota’s clinically relevant adult IRTS/RCS model is typically five to 15 beds and uses a prospective cost-based public-payment methodology.[^8]

This is a strategy and diligence framework, not legal, reimbursement, or valuation advice. Every candidate needs state counsel, a regulator pre-application discussion, contract review, and a facility-specific clinical and life-safety assessment before a conversion case is put into price.

## 1. The decision is three strategies, not one

The phrase “buy versus pivot” hides three materially different transactions.

| Strategy | What is actually being purchased | What validates it at the market-screen stage | Principal risk | Recommended role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Acquire core** | Existing adult primary-MH residential/subacute operations | Current licensed beds, adults served, primary-MH program, payer/referral evidence, current sites | Scarce exact-fit companies may be small or expensive | First platform and largest strategic focus |
| **B. Acquire adjacent and change mix** | Adult SUD/co-occurring or lower-acuity adult-MH platform plus convertible beds | Current residential footprint, co-occurring depth, site usability, state pathway, realistic bed-conversion range | New license, clinical model, contract, workforce, and census all must converge | Main source of larger mature-company choices |
| **C. Acquire site/entitlement** | Real estate, zoning, certificate/approval, or dormant beds | Exact approval scope, site control, life-safety readiness, bed authorization | Startup risk with no durable census, team, or revenue | Opportunistic market-entry lane |

The first acquisition should usually be Strategy A or a strong hybrid: a functioning core business with a bounded adjacent conversion opportunity. A pure pivot as the first deal forces the buyer to learn a new state, new level of care, new payer product, and new clinical operating model simultaneously. That is a concentration of unpriced risks, not a discount.

For the current Fifty States universe, the practical allocation rule should be:

- maintain a **core-acquisition lane** for existing adult psychiatric residential and step-down operators;
- maintain a separate **pivot-platform lane** for mature adult SUD/co-occurring and lower-acuity adult-MH companies;
- maintain an **approval/site lane** only where a permit, CON/DoN, zoning position, hospital relationship, or hard-to-replicate building is demonstrably reusable;
- never merge the three into one ranking score without preserving the reason a company is valuable.

The existing platform screen already contains 8,516 organization proposals, a 1,745-company broad consideration set, 1,067 mature-scale proposals, and 60 mature target-service signals. Forty parent companies have been manually organized into an initial evidence-backed/probable-100-bed consideration file.[^9] Those counts are discovery inventory, not proof of ownership, availability, capacity, payer mix, or pivotability.

## 2. What a pivot really changes

A credible adult high-acuity residential model is not simply “residential beds with mental-health programming.” The target product is closer to the model Georgia now defines in law: adults, primary psychiatric treatment, 24/7 operation, intensive short-term non-hospital care, and prevention of or transition from acute psychiatric hospitalization.[^3] SAMHSA similarly describes crisis residential services as short, voluntary residential care that can divert from or step down from inpatient hospitalization.[^10]

Changing an adjacent program into that product can require a simultaneous change across seven systems:

| System | What may carry over | What usually needs fresh proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **License and approved service** | Existing legal entity, compliance history, site records | Authority for adult primary psychiatric residential care, approved capacity, admissions criteria |
| **Physical plant** | Bedrooms, kitchen, common space, some life-safety systems | Observation design, controlled access, medication room, nursing station, anti-ligature and self-harm mitigation, privacy and clinical space |
| **Clinical workforce** | Counselors, technicians, nursing, utilization review | Psychiatric medical direction, psychiatric nursing, qualified mental-health professionals, case management, crisis competencies |
| **Clinical operations** | 24/7 scheduling, medication workflows, residential milieu | Suicide/self-harm, psychosis, aggression, elopement, involuntary-transfer, stabilization, and hospital-transfer protocols |
| **Payer** | Revenue-cycle infrastructure and some payer relationships | New level-of-care contract, rate, credentialing, authorization rules, medical-necessity criteria, claims testing |
| **Referral engine** | Brand, call center, community contacts | Hospital discharge flow, psychiatrist referrals, emergency/crisis relationships, proven conversion from referral to authorized admission |
| **Economics** | Current occupancy and current EBITDA | New net collected rate, authorized days, staffing cost, denial leakage, ramp, contribution margin, sustainable exit multiple |

Georgia’s rule illustrates why the distinction matters. The program must have a psychiatrist or physician, registered or advanced-practice nurse, clinical case-management staff, mental-health technicians or comparable staff, an on-site manager whenever operating, and continuous access to a physician or qualified mental-health professional. The rule also specifies a nurses’ station, medication room, surveillance considerations, clinical space, transfer arrangements, and written hospital referral agreements.[^3] An attractive residential building can still be the wrong clinical plant.

### Source-asset ranking

| Source business | Clinical adjacency | Site adjacency | Payer adjacency | Overall pivot view |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: | --- |
| Adult primary-MH residential, lower acuity | High | High | Medium | Best pivot; often an acuity expansion rather than a category change |
| Adult SUD residential with verified co-occurring program | Medium-high | High | Medium | Best adjacent acquisition; requires separate primary-MH authority and psychiatric operating model |
| Adult SUD residential without psychiatric depth | Medium | High | Low-medium | Site and 24/7 operating platform may transfer; clinical and contracting rebuild is substantial |
| Adult psychiatric hospital or unused hospital campus | High | Medium | Medium-high | Useful for step-down carve-out, but hospital asset cost and regulation can overwhelm the thesis |
| Adult supportive residence/personal-care model | Medium-low | Medium | Low | Residents and buildings may be adjacent; acuity, staffing, and payer model are usually not |
| Youth RTC/PRTF | Low | Medium | Low | Different population, benefit, safeguarding, staff model, and often public-payer economics |
| Recovery residence/sober living | Low | Medium-low | Low | Housing is not treatment; do not underwrite it as a clinical-license shortcut |
| Outpatient/OTP-only platform | Low | Low | Medium | Useful referral or payer infrastructure, but not a residential conversion platform |

SAMHSA’s co-occurring guidance supports integrated care and “no wrong door,” but it also says a provider should refer when a disorder is beyond its capacity and resources.[^1] Therefore, prior co-occurring experience is evidence of **organizational adjacency**, not evidence that the current program can safely accept a primary-psychosis or post-hospital population.

## 3. The asset-continuity problem

The acquisition thesis improves only when the buyer knows which assets survive the transaction. “Buy the license” is generally an unsafe shorthand.

### 3.1 Equity deals can preserve more continuity, but only in some states

Pennsylvania’s general human-services rule issues the certificate to a legal entity at a specific facility and makes it nontransferable. A change in the legal entity, name, location, or profit status voids the certificate. The same rule expressly says that a transfer of corporate stock does not constitute a change of ownership of the legal entity.[^2] That makes a stock transaction potentially valuable when the licensed subsidiary, location, service, and profit status remain intact.

But that is not the end of the analysis. The buyer still must test:

- whether the target is a corporation, LLC, nonprofit, or other entity to which the stock rule applies;
- change-of-control notice or approval under program-specific rules;
- payer-contract assignment and credentialing;
- Medicaid enrollment and tax-ID consequences;
- debt, leases, accreditation, controlled-substance registrations, laboratory arrangements, and local permits;
- successor liability and historical overpayment, quality, employment, and regulatory exposure.

Pennsylvania’s Medicaid instructions make the tax-ID distinction concrete: an ownership/control change under the same enrolled IRS number uses updated ownership disclosures, while a new tax ID requires a letter, transaction documents where applicable, and a new enrollment application for the relevant provider type.[^11]

### 3.2 In other states, the transaction triggers a new-license process

Georgia requires at least 30 days’ advance notice of an ownership change in the form of a license application that must be approved before the new owner receives a permit; permits are not transferable between programs or locations.[^3] Tennessee’s rules likewise treat a change of ownership or location as requiring a new license before services continue, while its administrative process allows an existing provider to request a new category, capacity change, relocation, renovation, or change in use.[^4]

Washington processes a private psychiatric hospital ownership change like an initial license and directs the applicant first through Certificate of Need and then construction review where applicable.[^12] Massachusetts requires initial and change-of-ownership applicants to satisfy applicable Determination of Need and plan review requirements before suitability, and it requires advance approval for location, beds, services, and renovations.[^13]

The correct transaction model is therefore a state-by-state **continuity matrix**, not a universal preference for equity or asset deals.

### 3.3 Referral agreements are necessary assets, not guaranteed volume

Georgia’s program requires admission through hospital discharge referral or a psychiatrist’s determination and requires written referral agreements with licensed general or psychiatric hospitals.[^3] Those relationships are strategically important, but diligence should separate:

- a legally compliant transfer/coordination agreement;
- a nonbinding relationship;
- a historical referral pattern;
- an enforceable volume commitment, if any;
- an unlawful payment or benefit tied to federally reimbursable referrals.

HHS-OIG explains that safe harbors protect defined arrangements that might otherwise implicate the federal Anti-Kickback Statute; arrangements outside a safe harbor require case-specific analysis.[^14] The acquisition case should therefore value a referral engine through verified historical admissions and lawful operational integration, not by capitalizing a promise to “send patients.”

## 4. Payer and reimbursement logic

### 4.1 Commercial parity helps, but it does not create a contract

Federal mental-health parity rules apply to intermediate levels of care and restrict plans from applying more stringent processes, standards, or factors to mental-health/substance-use benefits than to comparable medical/surgical benefits. Nonquantitative treatment limitations include medical-necessity rules, prior authorization, provider-network admission standards, reimbursement-rate methods, geography, facility type, and provider specialty.[^15]

That supports the long-term legitimacy of residential care as a benefit class. It does **not** guarantee that:

- a plan covers every residential program;
- a converted facility is admitted to a network;
- the payer accepts the requested daily rate;
- the payer authorizes the assumed length of stay;
- the program collects the contracted amount without denials or recoupments.

The Departments stated in May 2025 that they would not enforce the portions newly added by the 2024 final parity rule until resolution of the pending litigation plus 18 months, while the pre-existing 2013 rule and statutory comparative-analysis duties remain effective.[^16] The underwriting conclusion is conservative: parity is a compliance backdrop, not a revenue forecast.

Before treating commercial payer fit as proven, the buyer should have facility- and level-of-care-specific evidence for:

1. benefit classification;
2. credentialing and network path;
3. contracted or single-case rate;
4. expected authorized days by diagnosis and discharge source;
5. denial and appeal behavior;
6. patient-responsibility collection;
7. billing taxonomy, place of service, legal entity, tax ID, and NPI configuration;
8. contract treatment of ownership and service changes.

### 4.2 Medicaid is optional strategically, but it still changes the facility design

Federal Medicaid generally excludes federal reimbursement for residents ages 22–64 in an Institution for Mental Diseases. CMS describes limited exceptions for younger and older populations.[^17] The federal IMD definition generally captures institutions of more than 16 beds primarily engaged in diagnosing, treating, or caring for persons with mental diseases. States can obtain approved Section 1115 authority for short-term SMI/SED stays in qualifying IMDs, and managed-care programs have limited in-lieu-of-service pathways, but those are state- and contract-specific.[^18]

For a commercially oriented buyer, the consequences are:

- a 50-, 100-, or 150-bed psychiatric residential campus may be operationally attractive while offering less routine adult Medicaid flexibility;
- keeping beds under an arbitrary threshold is not a sound strategy if the sites are functionally one institution or the clinical/economic model requires more scale;
- any 16-bed pilot must be designed for clinical and operating reasons and confirmed with state/CMS counsel, not used as superficial IMD engineering;
- public-payer revenue should be modeled only where the state authority and MCO contract support it.

Pennsylvania and Minnesota show why license fit and payer fit must remain separate. Pennsylvania requires national accreditation for for-profit RTFA providers and describes Medicaid enrollment for RTFA as a supplemental service involving a co-occurring non-hospital rehabilitation/detox model and MCO contact.[^19] Minnesota explicitly says that an IRTS/RCS license does not guarantee Medicaid certification or accelerate rate setting, and each provider must contract with its funding sources.[^8]

## 5. State strategy

### Tier 1 — build the first two regulatory playbooks

#### Pennsylvania: best acquisition-continuity thesis

Pennsylvania licenses both Long-Term Structured Residences and Residential Treatment Facilities for Adults. LTSRs serve adults with severe psychosocial disability who are eligible for hospitalization but can be cared for in the residence; RTFAs provide highly structured psychiatric stabilization with an average stay of roughly 30 days.[^20]

Why it is attractive:

- the state already recognizes the exact conceptual space between community residence and hospital;
- a stock transaction may preserve the licensed legal entity under the general Chapter 20 rule;
- the RTFA instructions explicitly contemplate a dually licensed co-occurring non-hospital rehabilitation/detox model;
- county demand and discharge bottlenecks can be evidenced locally—for example, a Pennsylvania county plan reported an LTSR waitlist larger than the program’s beds and described delayed hospital discharges when residential placements were unavailable.[^21]

What can break the thesis:

- county support/acknowledgment is part of the new-license package;
- for-profit RTFA providers need national accreditation;
- Medicaid/MCO enrollment follows licensure and is not automatic;
- a new legal entity, site, name, or profit status can void the certificate;
- many LTSR/RTFA economics are public or mixed payer, not the desired commercial concentration.

**Recommended use:** find mature adult SUD/co-occurring or lower-acuity adult-MH companies whose licensed subsidiary and site can remain intact, then test an RTFA/core bed expansion. Prefer a stock or merger structure only when legal, tax, compliance, and payer diligence show that continuity is real. Pennsylvania is the strongest **acquire-and-expand** state, not necessarily the highest-rate state.

#### Georgia: best product-definition thesis

Georgia’s ARMHP rule became operationally mandatory on July 1, 2025. It defines a four-or-more-bed, adult, 24/7, subacute psychiatric residential alternative specifically intended to prevent or transition from acute hospitalization.[^3]

Why it is attractive:

- the license definition closely matches the desired adult high-acuity, non-hospital step-down product;
- admission pathways are tied to hospitals and psychiatrists rather than general housing demand;
- the rule allows charges and private agreements and excludes state/local public beds from the ARMHP definition;
- the current Fifty States evidence already contains regulator-verified adult ARMHP capacity, proving that private operators are obtaining this license.[^9]

What can break the thesis:

- the permit is location-specific and not transferable;
- new ownership requires advance application and approval;
- zoning, fire, occupancy, self-harm mitigation, clinical plant, and staffing must be proven;
- written hospital agreements are required, but no regulation guarantees admission volume or commercial reimbursement;
- because the license is new, mature operating and payer histories may be limited.

Georgia’s revised CON environment requires care. The ARMHP rule itself is a licensure rule, while Georgia asks parties that believe an activity is exempt from CON review to obtain a letter of determination before implementation.[^22] **Do not represent an ARMHP project as CON-exempt until the Office of Health Planning confirms the specific activity.**

**Recommended use:** make Georgia the first clean clinical playbook. Acquire an existing ARMHP where quality and payer evidence are acceptable, or acquire an adjacent adult SUD/co-occurring platform when its current residential footprint, sites, workforce, and operating history are independently useful. Condition any bed conversion on regulator and payer milestones.

### Tier 2 — operating-company and bolt-on markets

#### Tennessee: workable conversion, limited regulatory scarcity

Tennessee expressly licenses a “Mental Health Residential Treatment Program for Adults” providing 24-hour intensive, coordinated, structured services. The state distinguishes it from supportive residential, supportive living, and crisis stabilization.[^23] Existing operators use a published process to add a category, change bed capacity, establish or relocate a site, renovate, or change use/occupancy.[^4]

**Recommended use:** prioritize acquisitions where the target already has the right site, workforce, and adult residential operations. The license-change process makes a conversion legible, but because a new category can be requested, the seller should not receive a large premium merely for being in Tennessee. The moat must come from the site, reputation, workforce, payer access, or referrals.

#### Florida: large source pool, ambiguous primary-MH conversion economics

Florida separately describes long-term adult residential treatment facilities and public crisis stabilization/short-term residential programs. DCF says Short-Term Residential Treatment provides acute and immediately subacute 24/7 care for people who would otherwise require hospitalization; the governing rule chapter is titled “Public Mental Health Crisis Stabilization Units and Short-Term Residential Treatment Programs.”[^5]

AHCA’s adult residential-treatment facility pathway has five levels and requires evidence of the right to occupy, local zoning, fire and sanitation approvals, a program description, and an approved change-of-ownership application for a transfer of 51% or more.[^24]

**Recommended use:** use Florida aggressively for finding mature adult SUD, co-occurring, and existing adult-MH operating companies. Before classifying a facility as a pivot target, identify the exact current and proposed license, whether the proposed business belongs in AHCA’s long-term residential model or DCF’s public crisis system, and the actual commercial benefit/rate. Florida is a target-rich **company** market; it is not yet a uniformly proven **commercial primary-MH license** market.

### Tier 3 — acquire scarce approval rather than assume easy conversion

#### Connecticut

Connecticut’s CON process covers establishment of a new mental-health/substance-use facility and transfer of ownership of a health-care facility. The state’s 2024 facilities plan says establishment or transfer of a for-profit behavioral-health facility requires CON.[^25]

**Recommended use:** value an existing for-profit approval only after confirming that the proposed service and ownership change fit the approved scope. This can create a real entry barrier, but the CON is a closing condition and public-policy review, not a freely transferable asset.

#### Massachusetts

Massachusetts DoN review can apply to substantial capital expenditures, substantial service changes, original licensure, many ownership transfers, and site changes. Licensure also requires plan review and advance approval for beds, services, location, and renovations.[^13]

**Recommended use:** acquire an existing approved program or campus when the current operations justify the price. Treat a material service-mix change as a new regulatory project. DoN scarcity may protect incumbents, but it also limits rapid post-close repositioning.

#### Washington

Washington treats a private psychiatric hospital ownership change like an initial application and may require CON before construction review. Adding/eliminating services, changing beds, or changing buildings requires an amended license and a CON determination.[^12]

**Recommended use:** pursue only when acquiring an existing psychiatric hospital approval, a specifically exempt bed opportunity, or a strategic campus. It is better suited to an **approval acquisition** than an SUD-to-MH residential conversion.

### Tier 4 — do not force the thesis

#### North Carolina

North Carolina requires approval before changing service category, capacity, location, or ownership, and a 24-hour residential applicant must obtain local zoning and LME/MCO need review. The state also says private-pay facilities do not need an LME/MCO reimbursement contract.[^7] However, its clearly defined psychiatric residential treatment facility category is youth-focused, while adult psychiatric/substance-use hospital beds require a need determination and CON; no beds can be developed when the State Medical Facilities Plan shows no need.[^26]

**Recommended use:** consider a licensed psychiatric-hospital acquisition or a facility-specific adult category confirmed by the regulator. Do not infer a scalable adult non-hospital psychiatric-residential license from generic 24-hour residential listings.

#### Minnesota

Minnesota IRTS/RCS is clinically aligned with stabilization and hospital diversion/step-down, but programs generally range from five to 15 beds. Rates are provider-specific and prospectively cost based, including direct service, program, and physical-plant cost components.[^8]

**Recommended use:** keep Minnesota as a public-payer/clinical-model reference and a possible small bolt-on market. It does not currently support the desired thesis of a concentrated, commercially oriented 50–150-bed acquisition platform.

## 6. Zoning and real-estate moat

Zoning can be valuable, but “grandfathered behavioral-health use” is not automatically durable or unique.

DOJ and HUD state that the Fair Housing Act applies to municipal land-use decisions, bars disability-based exclusion, and can require reasonable accommodation for group homes. They also make clear that neutral health, safety, occupancy, and land-use requirements may apply and that a large institutional facility can fundamentally alter a single-family district even when a small group home would not.[^27]

The acquisition screen should therefore distinguish five different real-estate assets:

1. **by-right use** for the exact current and proposed occupancy;
2. **conditional/special-use approval** that survives the ownership and service change;
3. **nonconforming/grandfathered use** with documented continuation rules;
4. **reasonable-accommodation position** for a dwelling-like group home;
5. **institutional/clinical occupancy approval** for a larger high-acuity facility.

The strongest site moat is a building with documented use rights for the proposed service, current fire and occupancy approvals, an acceptable life-safety configuration, expansion capacity, hospital access, and no ownership/change-of-use trigger. A favorable planning official’s email or the fact that another residential use operated there is not enough.

## 7. Market environment, bed scale, and similar entities

The market is much larger than the small number of fully verified core operators suggests. The current non-California source contains 18,742 facility records and 8,516 proposed organizations. The problem is not a shortage of names. It is that most public records do not cleanly distinguish a parent company’s adult primary-MH beds from SUD, hospital, youth, IDD, eating-disorder, outpatient, recovery-housing, and public community-residential capacity.[^9]

The practical market has four layers:

1. **Exact-fit operators:** adult primary-MH residential, subacute, crisis-step-down, or close equivalents. These are the best operating analogs but are often small and fragmented.
2. **Near-fit operators:** adult residential SUD/co-occurring or mixed behavioral-health platforms. These create the largest mature-company acquisition pool and the most plausible convertible-bed inventory.
3. **Hospital platforms:** large, visible bed counts but a different license, staffing, reimbursement, capital, and transaction model.
4. **Public/community models:** clinically informative but often small-bed, cost-based, county/state-supported, or Medicaid-dependent.

### 7.1 Bed bands should describe strategic scale

Use the following bands as screening ranges, not as claims of exact licensed capacity:

| Non-California relevant-bed band | Strategic interpretation for a 500-bed buyer | Typical use |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| **Under 25** | Clinical archetype or small campus; too small to establish a new regional platform alone | Program analog, tuck-in, management or clinical-team acquisition |
| **25–49** | Meaningful single facility or several small homes | Local bolt-on or contained conversion pilot |
| **50–99** | Subscale company but potentially important if fit is unusually strong | Regional foothold, high-fit exception, add-on platform |
| **100–249** | Best initial target band: large enough to matter, still potentially segmentable and locally managed | Regional platform or first material acquisition |
| **250–499** | Substantial platform; more likely to include mixed services, states, and payer models | Multi-state platform or major adjacent acquisition |
| **500–999** | Large platform; target-fit beds are rarely the entire total | Selective platform acquisition, carve-out, or strategic partnership |
| **1,000+** | Usually hospital-heavy, national, or highly diversified | Corporate-scale transaction; poor proxy for usable target beds |

For every company, maintain three capacity numbers rather than one:

- **documented current capacity:** current regulator or equally authoritative beds/slots;
- **published lower bound:** current first-party or other credible capacity that is not a full license sum;
- **screening total range:** a directional parent-level band before all facilities are reconciled.

Then split each into:

- current core adult primary-MH beds;
- adjacent adult SUD/co-occurring or lower-acuity beds;
- hospital/youth/IDD/eating-disorder/nonresidential beds;
- realistically convertible beds, which must be lower than or equal to adjacent beds and supported by a site/license review.

That is why a rigid “100 verified core beds or nothing” rule would lose relevant targets. The right screen preserves high-fit sub-100 operators as add-ons and operating comparables while separately identifying larger parents with convertible segments.

### 7.3 Mature adjacent platforms: the main acquisition supply

These are not direct adult primary-MH comparables. They are the closest large-company source pool because they already operate adult residential care, manage utilization, and often treat co-occurring disorders.[^9]

| Similar entity | General non-California capacity signal | Market-fit view | What must be separated |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| **Recovery Centers of America** | At least **344 regulator-verified Massachusetts beds**; company total is higher | Large, mature adult residential SUD/co-occurring platform | State-by-state residential beds, exact primary-MH capability, payer mix |
| **EOSIS** | At least **518 published residential beds** in Minnesota | Strong residential scale and co-occurring experience | Public/mixed payer orientation, duplicate/co-located programs, licensed versus staffed beds |
| **Advanced Recovery Systems** | At least **152 verified beds before its 2026 Promises acquisition**; actual total materially higher | One of the most relevant scaled pivot-platform archetypes because it spans SUD, co-occurring, and some primary MH | Rebuild the combined portfolio and distinguish current core from adjacent beds |
| **Gain Holdings Group / WhiteSands** | More than **180 published beds** across two Florida campuses | Mature single-state adult detox/residential operator with commercial/mixed signal | Parent/legal-entity rollup, licensed capacity, and primary-MH pathway |
| **Boca Recovery Center** | At least **126 published beds** across three facilities; total higher | Regional commercial/mixed adult residential platform | Add remaining inpatient sites and verify licensed capacity |
| **Northpoint Recovery** | At least **108 published beds** across two campuses; total higher | Mature regional adult inpatient SUD/co-occurring platform | Separate adult inpatient from adolescent and outpatient programs |
| **Bradford Health Services** | **500–999 estimated** | Large mature regional SUD source platform | Exact active residential beds and commercial versus public mix |
| **Banyan Treatment Centers** | **250–499 estimated** | Multi-state adult MH/SUD, detox, and residential source platform | Licensed residential capacity and mental-health-only segments |
| **Avenues Recovery Center** | **250–499 estimated** | Multi-state adult detox/residential/co-occurring source platform | Current licensed beds across the 18-facility footprint |
| **Meadows Behavioral Healthcare** | **100–249 estimated relevant beds** | Strong commercial trauma/MH/SUD specialty adjacency | Residential sites must be separated from outpatient and non-core specialty programs |

This is the pool most likely to contain mature management teams, multiple campuses, and 100–500 relevant residential beds. It is also where false precision is most dangerous. A 500-bed parent may yield only 50–150 realistically usable target beds after adult status, primary diagnosis, license, state, building, and payer segmentation.

### 7.4 Hospital entities belong in a separate comparison set

Large psychiatric-hospital bed counts should not make a company rank ahead of a smaller residential operator. Current examples include Signature Healthcare Services with 1,307 listed non-California hospital beds, SUN Behavioral Health with 579 hospital beds, and Oceans Healthcare with at least 230 beds in a partial hospital slice.[^9]

These entities matter for three reasons:

- they identify hospital referral and discharge markets;
- they may own campuses or separable buildings suitable for step-down development;
- they show the scale of acute psychiatric infrastructure surrounding the target product.

They are not clean comparables for non-hospital adult residential care. Keep hospital beds, residential beds, and realistically convertible campus capacity in separate columns.

### 7.5 Market-fit hierarchy

Rank the environment in this order:

1. **Adult primary-MH fit:** the program treats psychiatric illness as primary, not merely co-occurring.
2. **Acuity fit:** accepts hospital diversion/step-down, SMI, psychosis, mood instability, or comparable high-needs populations within a non-hospital scope.
3. **Residential fit:** staffed 24/7 treatment beds, not housing, outpatient service, or a marketing service area.
4. **Payer fit:** commercial or mixed payer with credible network/authorization access; public revenue may remain but should not be the only viable source.
5. **Scale fit:** enough current or convertible beds to matter, while retaining the ability to segment populations safely.
6. **Market-entry fit:** a state/site/license position that is difficult enough to reproduce to justify acquisition rather than greenfield development.
7. **Maturity fit:** multi-site operating discipline, current compliance systems, experienced leadership, and a stable referral engine.

The market thesis should therefore prefer a 75-bed exact-fit regional operator over a 700-bed diversified parent when the latter contains only a small, unverified adult residential segment. It should prefer the larger parent only when the relevant bed segment and conversion pathway can be isolated.

### 7.6 Valuation boundary

The supplied spreadsheet formulas remain useful later, but their rates, occupancy, margins, and multiples are placeholders rather than verified company results.[^28][^29] At this stage, retain only two rules:

- do not convert a screening bed range into a company valuation;
- do not count proposed future or hypothetically convertible beds as current capacity.

The immediate product should answer **who operates where, approximately how many relevant beds they control, what type of beds those are, and how closely the business matches the target model**. Detailed valuation belongs after the owner selects the 50–100-company research cohort and the top candidates receive license-level bed verification.

## 8. Recommended phased-conversion design

The safest conversion is a **contained unit or campus**, not a company-wide rebrand.

### Phase 0 — close on the as-is business

Conditions:

- current license, capacity, ownership, quality history, and financials verified;
- purchase price works without conversion;
- payer and referral contracts reviewed for control/assignment effects;
- site and lease survive the transaction;
- no assumption that current SUD or supportive beds are psychiatric beds.

### Phase 1 — regulatory and payer proof

Before capital construction:

- written state determination of the target service/license path;
- local zoning, occupancy, fire, and construction determination;
- facility-specific clinical gap assessment;
- payer term sheets or written contracting path for the exact level of care;
- hospital/psychiatrist referral validation using historical discharge volume and interviews;
- detailed unit economics based on net collections and required staffing.

### Phase 2 — bounded pilot

Convert a physically and clinically separable unit or small campus—often 16 to 24 beds for operational learning, but sized according to the actual state license, building, and payer case. Do not mix incompatible populations merely to preserve occupancy. Keep the adjacent operation running while the new unit proves:

- authorized admissions and days;
- net collected revenue per occupied bed-day;
- denial, appeal, and recoupment rates;
- referral-to-admission conversion;
- time from referral to admission;
- occupancy and length of stay;
- direct-care and clinical labor hours per occupied day;
- medication, transfer, elopement, restraint, injury, and serious-incident performance;
- contribution margin after corporate allocations;
- discharge destination, readmission, and emergency-transfer outcomes.

### Phase 3 — scale only after repeatability

Scale when the pilot has demonstrated a reproducible referral source, payer authorization behavior, safe staffing, stable quality, and collected—not billed—economics. Expansion should be a fresh capital decision, not the automatic fulfillment of the acquisition model.

## 9. Acquisition diligence gates

### No-go gates

A candidate should not advance as a high-fit pivot target if any of the following remains unresolved:

- no confirmed adult primary-MH residential license category;
- license or CON/DoN scope will not survive or cannot be reissued on the transaction timeline;
- proposed use is incompatible with zoning, occupancy, fire, or building rules;
- the plant cannot support observation, medication, privacy, and self-harm mitigation at a rational cost;
- no credible psychiatric medical director, nursing, clinical leadership, and recruitment plan;
- no written payer path for the exact level of care;
- referral case is based on general “need” rather than accessible discharges and authorized admissions;
- purchase price requires the full pivot to work;
- current compliance liabilities cannot be bounded.

### Diligence evidence ladder

| Confidence | Required evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Low** | Company marketing, directory listing, broker claim, generic payer relationship, generic state license label |
| **Medium** | Current license record, current site/capacity, verified legal operator, preliminary regulator response, historical claims and referrals |
| **High** | Exact license/service crosswalk, written CHOW/service-change path, facility plans, payer contract/rate or binding process, collected claims, validated staffing model, current quality record |
| **Transaction-ready** | High-confidence evidence plus confirmed relevant-bed count, facility-level conversion plan, responsible executive, milestone calendar, and bounded operating risks |

### Recommended scoring model

Use hard gates first. For candidates that pass, score market fit and pivotability out of 100:

- 20 — source clinical adjacency;
- 15 — physical-plant and life-safety feasibility;
- 15 — regulator-confirmed target-license path;
- 10 — transaction/license continuity;
- 15 — payer path and credible collected-rate evidence;
- 10 — accessible hospital/psychiatrist referral flow;
- 10 — workforce availability and leadership;
- 5 — working-capital capacity and conversion runway.

Keep this score separate from raw company size. A highly pivotable facility can still be too small or operationally weak; a large operating company can contain very few usable target beds.

## 11. Immediate research program

The next stage should not research all 8,516 proposals. It should produce a 25–40-company **pivot-platform cohort** next to the existing core cohort.

### Workstream 1 — regulatory crosswalk

For Pennsylvania, Georgia, Tennessee, and Florida:

1. map every current adjacent license to the exact desired target license;
2. document CHOW, service-addition, capacity, site, accreditation, and CON/DoN steps;
3. obtain written regulator or counsel confirmation for the top two common conversions;
4. identify whether one legal entity can operate both programs at the same site;
5. document the unit-separation and building requirements.

### Workstream 2 — candidate construction

Start with mature adult SUD/co-occurring and lower-acuity adult-MH parents in the existing consideration file. The most relevant archetypes are:

- multi-site adult detox/residential SUD with psychiatric and co-occurring depth;
- an adult mental-health operator with lower-acuity/supportive beds and unused site capacity;
- a mixed behavioral-health parent with one underutilized campus suitable for a contained pilot;
- a psychiatric-hospital operator with a separable non-hospital campus or building, not an expensive acute hospital that must be bought whole.

Exclude or hold:

- youth-only platforms;
- recovery housing without treatment authority;
- outpatient/OTP-heavy companies whose residential assets are immaterial;
- companies whose capacity is mostly hospital, IDD, eating disorder, or Medicaid-funded community residence unless the specific carve-out is proven;
- any target whose “100+ beds” is only a brand claim without current license reconciliation.

### Workstream 3 — payer/referral proof before target research becomes expensive

For each target state, interview or obtain data from:

- two or more commercial behavioral-health payers or network managers;
- three to five psychiatric hospitals/discharge teams;
- major psychiatry groups and crisis providers;
- existing adult residential operators;
- state licensing and health-planning staff.

The questions are specific: exact covered level of care, network need, reimbursement structure, authorization days, exclusion criteria, discharge volumes, rejected referrals, reasons for delayed discharge, and minimum facility requirements. General statements that “beds are needed” do not clear the gate.

### Workstream 4 — first pilot selection

Choose the first pivot only if:

- the acquisition stands on as-is economics;
- the unit can be physically separated;
- the state path is written;
- a payer path is documented;
- a hospital/referral source will support launch;
- an experienced psychiatric residential executive owns the conversion;
- the downside case preserves liquidity.

## 12. Final recommendation

The platform should pursue **acquisition-led entry with selective mix change**, not a broad pivot of existing beds.

The preferred sequence is:

1. acquire or partner with one proven adult primary-MH residential operator in Pennsylvania or Georgia;
2. use that platform to establish clinical protocols, payer evidence, hospital relationships, and a regulator-tested opening playbook;
3. buy mature adult SUD/co-occurring bolt-ons in Pennsylvania, Georgia, Tennessee, or Florida when the current residential footprint, sites, team, and realistically convertible bed range are useful even before the full pivot;
4. convert one contained unit at a time after written regulatory and payer milestones;
5. use Connecticut, Massachusetts, and Washington for approval-driven acquisitions only when the scarcity justifies the added time and closing risk;
6. avoid forcing Minnesota and North Carolina into the commercial non-hospital model until a specific license and payer path is proved.

The deeper strategic insight is that the valuable acquisition target is not merely a company with beds. It is a **continuity package**:

> adult-appropriate site + usable approval path + credible current and convertible bed count + psychiatric operating capability + lawful referral access + collectible payer contract

If any one of those elements must be created after closing, it is a buyer-funded option and should be priced, gated, and managed as such.
